Introduction
Municipal water networks in mid-sized cities are among the oldest pieces of infrastructure still in daily use, and in many of them the pipes that carry drinking water under the streets were laid before the invention of the devices now used to monitor them. The operators of such networks face a persistent and expensive problem: water leaves the treatment works in a known quantity, but a substantial fraction of it, often between a fifth and a third, never reaches a customer meter. Some of this loss is accounted for by firefighting, mains flushing and unmetered public use, but the larger share escapes through leaks that are too small to surface and too numerous to find by inspection. In this report we describe a three-year study, carried out in partnership with the water utility of a city of roughly four hundred thousand inhabitants, in which a dense network of low-cost acoustic and pressure sensors was installed across one supply district and used to locate leaks, to estimate the volume lost through them, and to test whether the information could be turned into a repair schedule that paid for itself. The district chosen for the study covers about eleven square kilometres, contains one hundred and ninety kilometres of mains of mixed age and material, and serves some thirty-one thousand connections, which makes it typical of the city as a whole and, we believe, of many other cities built up during the same period.
The sensing approach rests on two complementary signals. The first is acoustic: a leak in a pressurised pipe produces a continuous broadband noise that travels along the pipe wall and through the water column, and a sensor clamped to a valve or hydrant can detect that noise at a distance of several tens of metres in metal pipe and somewhat less in plastic. The second is hydraulic: when the demand in a district is lowest, in the early hours of the morning, the flow entering the district should fall to a floor set by legitimate night-time use, and any excess above that floor is a measure of the leakage present. By combining night-flow analysis, which tells the operator how much is being lost in a district, with acoustic correlation, which tells the operator where along a pipe the loss is occurring, the study aimed to move the utility from a reactive posture, in which leaks are repaired when they become visible, to one in which hidden leaks are located and ranked by size before any water reaches the surface. The sensors were designed by the project team around a commodity microcontroller, a piezoelectric accelerometer, a pressure transducer and a low-power radio, and each unit cost about a sixth of the price of the commercial loggers the utility had previously used in small numbers. Three hundred and forty units were deployed, giving an average spacing of a little over five hundred metres along the mains, with closer spacing in the older parts of the district where the pipes are cast iron and the historical burst rate is highest.
Installation took four months and was carried out by the utility's own field crews after a week of training. Each sensor was fixed to the spindle of a valve or the outlet of a hydrant inside its existing chamber, so that no excavation was needed, and each unit was configured to record a short acoustic sample every fifteen minutes during the night, to log pressure continuously at one reading per second, and to transmit a compressed summary to a gateway once an hour. The gateways, of which there were nineteen, were mounted on lamp posts and powered from the street lighting supply, and they forwarded the data over the cellular network to a server at the utility's headquarters. The first weeks of operation revealed several practical difficulties that we document in full in the body of the report. Chambers that flooded after heavy rain drowned the radios of a dozen units until a revised antenna mounting was introduced. Traffic noise from a trunk road produced a daily pattern of false positives in the acoustic channel that was removed only when the processing was restricted to the hours between two and four in the morning. And the batteries, which had been specified for a five-year life, proved sensitive to the low winter temperatures inside shallow chambers, so that the sampling schedule had to be thinned during the coldest months. None of these problems was fatal, but together they consumed most of the project's first year, and we recommend that any similar deployment budget at least as long for the shakedown period as for the installation itself.
The analysis pipeline was built in three stages. In the first stage, each sensor's acoustic samples were reduced to a small set of spectral features and compared with that sensor's own long-term baseline, so that a sustained rise in noise at a single location raised a flag without any assumption about the pipe material or the surrounding ground. In the second stage, flagged sensors were paired with their neighbours and the arrival time difference of the correlated noise was used to estimate the position of the source along the pipe between them, using the known pipe length and a propagation speed drawn from a lookup table by material and diameter. In the third stage, the estimated positions were combined with the pressure records to produce a ranked list of suspected leaks, with each entry carrying an estimated flow rate derived from the local pressure, the pipe size and an empirical relationship between leak noise level and orifice area that was calibrated during the study against leaks that were subsequently excavated and measured. The utility's leakage engineers reviewed this list weekly and chose which entries to investigate with a conventional ground microphone before committing a repair crew.
Over the thirty months of full operation the system flagged four hundred and twelve suspected leaks, of which three hundred and one were confirmed on investigation, eighty-four were found to be something else, most commonly a passing valve or a customer-side leak beyond the meter, and twenty-seven could not be resolved either way. The confirmed leaks ranged from weeping joints losing a few litres an hour to a fractured main losing more than forty cubic metres a day, and their estimated positions were within three metres of the excavated point in four cases out of five, which is close enough that a repair crew can open the ground once rather than twice. Measured against the night-flow record, the district's leakage fell from an estimated twenty-eight per cent of the water supplied at the start of the study to just under fifteen per cent at its end, and the utility's own accounting attributes about two thirds of that reduction to repairs that would not have been made, or would have been made much later, without the sensor network. The cost of the water saved, valued at the marginal cost of treatment and pumping, exceeded the combined capital and operating cost of the network within twenty months, and the saving continues to accrue. We also report a less expected benefit: the continuous pressure record made it possible to identify three pressure-reducing valves that were set too high and two that were failing, and adjusting them reduced the burst rate in the oldest part of the district by a margin that we estimate to be worth as much again as the leak detection.
To separate the effect of the sensor network from the ordinary year-to-year variation in leakage, the utility agreed at the outset to treat a neighbouring district of similar size, age and pipe material as a control. That district received no sensors and continued to be managed in the usual way, with leaks repaired as they surfaced and a night-flow reading taken once a quarter at the district meter. Over the same thirty months its leakage moved from an estimated twenty-six per cent to twenty-four, a change within the range seen in previous years and well short of the thirteen-point fall in the study district. The two districts experienced the same winters, the same pressure regime at the trunk main and the same crew availability, since the repair gangs were shared between them, so we are reasonably confident that the difference is attributable to the information the sensors provided rather than to any change in the weather or in the utility's resources. We did find one confounding effect that we could not fully remove: once the engineers had learned to read the sensor output they became more skilled at interpreting conventional ground-microphone surveys as well, and some of that skill will have been applied in the control district during the later part of the study. If anything, this biases the comparison against the sensor network, and the true effect may be slightly larger than the figure we report. The full statistical treatment, including the uncertainty on the night-flow estimates and the sensitivity of the result to the choice of baseline period, is given in an appendix, together with the monthly leakage series for both districts.
The report closes with a discussion of what would be needed to extend the approach across the whole city and to other utilities. The hardware is not the obstacle; the units proved reliable once the early faults were corrected, and their cost continues to fall. The limiting factor is the capacity of the leakage engineers to act on the information, since a ranked list of several hundred suspected leaks is only useful if there are enough crews to investigate and repair them, and we found that the system's value was greatest when the utility treated it as a scheduling tool rather than as an alarm. We also note that the calibration between noise level and leak flow is specific to the pipe materials and soil types of the district, and would need to be repeated elsewhere, and that the night-flow method depends on the district being hydraulically isolated, which is not the case everywhere. Nevertheless, the central result stands: in a district representative of much of the city, a sensor network built from low-cost components and installed without excavation halved the leakage within three years, paid for itself well inside that period, and left the utility with a continuous picture of its network that it had never had before. The data, the sensor design files and the analysis code are released with the report so that other utilities can repeat the study on their own networks and set their own results beside the ones reported here.
